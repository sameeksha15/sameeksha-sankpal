I have attended lectures. I have sat through seminars. I have watched countless hours of physics content online. But nothing, *nothing*, quite prepares you for the moment you are standing a few metres away from a living legend of your field and your brain short-circuits trying to process that this person is real.

That was me, on the evening of May 26th, at the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research.

## Getting in

The gates opened around 4 PM. We were handed wristbands at the entrance, the kind that make you feel like you have backstage passes to the most exciting show in town (which, honestly, you do). Then we filed into the **Homi Bhabha Auditorium**.

If you have not been, the auditorium is *massive*. About 800 people filled it that evening: students, researchers, professors, curious minds of every kind, all there for one reason. The air was electric with the kind of excitement you usually only feel before a cricket match. Except this time, the pitch was quantum mechanics.

## Why this evening was special, twice over

This was not just any public lecture. It was the inaugural event of the **Lodha Theoretical Physics Institute (LTPI)**, a new institute dedicated to fundamental research in theoretical physics, right here in Mumbai.

TIFR, of course, needs no introduction. For over 75 years it has been the beating heart of fundamental scientific research in India. Founded with the vision of a young, independent nation that dared to dream big about science, TIFR has shaped entire generations of physicists, mathematicians, and researchers. It laid the intellectual foundation that many of India's scientific achievements stand on today.

LTPI is that vision, expanded and carried forward. Its Founding Director is **Prof. Jainendra Jain**, Evan Pugh University Professor and Eberly Chair in Physics at Penn State, and one of the foremost theoretical physicists in the world. Prof. Jain has long wanted to contribute to Indian science in a sustained, deeper way, and LTPI is, in his own words, *"a dream realised."* The institute aims to attract world-class faculty and exceptional postdoctoral researchers, and to build a vibrant visitors' programme: a place designed, as he puts it, to *spawn bold ideas and seminal, transformative discoveries.*

To mark the launch, LTPI is hosting **EPQHS-10**, the 10th edition of the international workshop on *Emergent Phenomena in Quantum Hall Systems*, bringing together some of the greatest physicists on the planet, in Mumbai, from May 27–29, 2026.

And the public lecture kicking off the whole celebration? Delivered by none other than a Nobel laureate.

## The stage is set

**Prof. Mandar Deshmukh**, experimental physicist at TIFR and a brilliant host, opened the evening with warmth and energy that immediately set the tone: this was going to be fun.

Then came the introductory remarks from **Ashish Kumar Singh**, Chief Mentor of the Lodha Foundation, which is behind the establishment of LTPI. He was measured, articulate, and genuinely thoughtful, speaking about India's scientific journey, the importance of fundamental research, and the philosophy of the Lodha Foundation: *"nurturing excellence and strengthening scientific capacity."* No fluff, no corporate-speak, just a clear, sincere vision for what science in India can become.

The foundation has committed to establishing LTPI as a world-class centre with internationally competitive standards of academic excellence, integrity, and independence. Listening to him speak, it felt real. It felt important.

But now, the moment everyone had been waiting for.

## The man himself

**Prof. Dr. Klaus von Klitzing** walked up to the dais.

I will be honest: when you picture a physicist of this stature, you might brace yourself for an hour of dense equations delivered in a monotone. A legend, yes, but perhaps a dry one.

Reader, I was completely wrong.

Prof. von Klitzing is 82 years old, a Nobel laureate, Director Emeritus of the Max Planck Institute for Solid State Research, and he has the energy, the wit, and the sheer joy of a person who has never stopped being delighted by physics. He was funny. He was warm. He told stories. He made the whole auditorium laugh. He made the whole auditorium *think*. And somehow, without once making you feel like you needed a PhD to keep up, he walked 800 people through one of the most profound discoveries in modern physics.

The lecture was titled **"Why Quantum Research Matters: From Curiosity to Real-World Impact"**, and it delivered on every word of that title.

## The quantum Hall effect, told like a story

The **integer quantum Hall effect** was discovered by Prof. von Klitzing in 1980. At its core it came from a deceptively simple experiment: cool electrons down to extremely low temperatures, squeeze them into a thin 2D layer, and apply a strong magnetic field. What happens to their electrical resistance?

The answer was supposed to be boring. It was not.

The resistance did not vary smoothly. It jumped, in perfectly precise, discrete steps. And not just approximately precise. *Perfectly* precise. The same value, every single time, in every single lab, anywhere on Earth. The precision was so extraordinary that it did not just win him the Nobel Prize in 1985; it eventually *redefined* how we measure electrical resistance globally. The von Klitzing constant, *R*<sub>K</sub>, is now a fixed constant in the international system of units. Prof. von Klitzing is one of only two living scientists to have a fundamental constant named after them.

Think about that for a moment. A physicist did an experiment out of pure curiosity about electrons behaving strangely. And it ended up changing how humanity measures the world.

That is what fundamental research *does*.

## The panel: a room full of giants

After the lecture came a Q&A panel that was, frankly, a little surreal. Seated together on stage were:

- **Prof. Klaus von Klitzing**, Max Planck Institute, Stuttgart
- **Prof. Bertrand Halperin**, Harvard University
- **Prof. Allan MacDonald**, University of Texas, Austin
- **Prof. Sankar Das Sarma**, University of Maryland

If you follow condensed matter physics or quantum Hall physics at all, these are names you have read in papers, cited in your own work, argued about with friends at 2 AM. Seeing them in conversation, bantering, disagreeing, building on each other's ideas, was like watching the field *think* in real time.

The questions from the audience were genuinely excellent too. Curious, probing, sometimes cheeky. The panel took them all in stride with the kind of ease that only comes from having spent decades thinking about hard problems.

## What I carried home

I came home that night with a full notebook and a head buzzing with ideas. But more than the physics, what struck me was the *feeling* in that auditorium.

Eight hundred people, many of them young students, completely locked in. Laughing at a Nobel laureate's jokes. Leaning forward when he described an experiment. Applauding not out of politeness but out of genuine excitement. This is what science can feel like when it is communicated with honesty, with humility, and with joy.

TIFR has been doing this for 75 years. If LTPI carries even a fraction of that spirit forward (and from everything I saw that evening, it will), Indian physics is in very good hands.

And for me personally? I walked in as someone who loves quantum physics. I walked out as someone who was reminded, all over again, *why.*
